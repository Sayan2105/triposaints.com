Alleppey

Where the roads are made of water

The Kuttanad backwaters

01Story

A port built for cargo, now carrying visitors

Alappuzha was built to move things. A nineteenth-century port with a pier reaching into the Arabian Sea and canals cut inland, it shipped coir, pepper and rice out of Kerala's wettest country. The old warehouses still stand — some are hotels now, most are still full of coconut fibre.

Behind the town lies Kuttanad, one of the few places on Earth where people farm below sea level: paddy fields held back from Vembanad Lake by low mud bunds and a great deal of stubbornness. The houseboats you came for are converted rice barges. They used to carry the harvest.

02Culture

Everything is arranged along the water

Ferries do the work of buses, children go to school by canoe, and most villages keep a rowing club. In the second week of August the snake boats race for the Nehru Trophy on Punnamada Lake — well over a hundred rowers to a boat, and an entire district taking sides. The sport never quite left the temple calendar: the oldest race of the season, at Champakulam each June or July, marks the day the idol of the Ambalappuzha Sri Krishna Temple was carried up these very canals.

Eat karimeen pollichathu, pearl spot wrapped in banana leaf and grilled, and Kuttanad duck roast, which is a local institution. The toddy shops sell fresh palm sap and some of the best fish curry in the state. They are not smart places, and that is rather the point. And fourteen kilometres south, that same Ambalappuzha temple has served palpayasam — milk and rice reduced for hours to something faintly caramel — for centuries; people ride the ferry just for a share.

03Practical guidance

The honest logistics

  • Best monthsNovember to February. The June–August monsoon is genuinely heavy; September–October is green, wet and much quieter.
  • Getting thereKochi airport (COK) is about three hours north by road. Alappuzha is on the coastal rail line, with direct trains from Ernakulam and Thiruvananthapuram.
  • StayingOne night afloat, the rest in a lakeside homestay or a converted warehouse in town — better food and better sleep.
  • Days neededTwo. One night on the water and one day of canals and Kuttanad is the honest amount.
  • Budget feelHomestays from ₹2,500/day; a private houseboat runs ₹8,000–₹15,000 a night for the whole boat.
04Local insight

What a Saint will tell you

A houseboat is a floating hotel, not a voyage — they moor before dark by rule, and the large ones can only use the wide channels, where all the others are. Spend a morning in a shikara canoe instead: the narrow canals behind the villages are where the place actually is. Book the boat first, then settle the food separately. That is where quality varies.

05Responsible travel

Being a good guest

Vembanad is a working lake and a protected wetland with several hundred houseboats on it. Ask your operator out loud whether the boat has a sewage holding tank — the question changes behaviour. Nothing goes over the side. And keep the music down: sound travels a long way over water, and people live here.
