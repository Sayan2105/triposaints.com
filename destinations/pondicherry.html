Pondicherry

A French accent in a Tamil town

White Town, Pondicherry

01Story

Two towns with a canal between them

France held this stretch of the Coromandel coast on and off for close to three hundred years and did not hand it over until 1954 — recent enough that some pensioners here still carry French passports. The town was laid out on a grid split by a canal: Ville Blanche on the sea side, Ville Noire inland.

The division survives in the buildings rather than the people. Bougainvillea over compound walls and street names in three scripts on one side; Tamil houses with thinnai verandahs and a market that opens at five on the other. Both halves are Pondicherry. Only one of them is on the postcards.

02Culture

The Ashram sets the clock

Sri Aurobindo's ashram has sat at the centre of White Town since 1926, and it still sets the rhythm. Many of the guesthouses, bookshops and dining halls belong to it, which is part of why the quarter goes quiet early and why the seafront road closes to traffic each evening and fills with walkers instead. It is not the quarter's oldest institution, though: the Manakula Vinayagar temple, a couple of streets inland, was standing before the French arrived, outlasted several colonial attempts to remove its Ganesha, and still throws White Town's loudest festival each Ganesh Chaturthi, in August or September.

Ten kilometres north, Auroville has been attempting since 1968 to be a township with no money and no nationality. It is neither the utopia its literature claims nor the fraud its critics insist on. Go, look at the Matrimandir from the viewpoint, eat at a community kitchen, and decide for yourself.

03Practical guidance

The honest logistics

  • Best monthsDecember to March. The northeast monsoon soaks October and November; April to June is hot and close.
  • Getting thereChennai (MAA) is the airport, about three and a half hours down the East Coast Road. By rail, most trains stop at Villupuram, 35 km inland.
  • StayingRestored heritage guesthouses in White Town; the Tamil quarter is cheaper and more awake. Auroville suits longer stays.
  • Days neededTwo days covers the town; four if you want Auroville and the beaches without rushing.
  • Budget feelComfortable from ₹2,500/day; heritage guesthouses from ₹7,000/day.
04Local insight

What a Saint will tell you

Promenade Beach is a seawall, not a beach — you cannot swim there, and the walk is best at six in the morning when the whole town is out on it. For sand, head north to Serenity or take the boat across to Paradise Beach. Eat one French meal and then stop; the better cooking here is Tamil, and the Creole kitchens near Vysial Street are the actual local cuisine.

05Responsible travel

Being a good guest

This coastline is eroding and the seawall is part of the reason — stay off the rocks, and off the fishing hamlets' working beaches at dawn. Pondicherry restricted single-use plastic before most of India did; honour it. And Auroville is somebody's home, not an attraction: book a visit rather than wandering in.

06Next journey

Where this path continues

Pondicherry's Tamil half is the half worth following inland. Madurai, a solid six hours south-west, is that culture at full concentration — a city mapped in squares around the Meenakshi temple, where the last procession ends near ten and the gates reopen before dawn. No French accent survives the drive.
