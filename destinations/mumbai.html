Mumbai

A city built by people who came from somewhere else

Marine Drive at dusk

01Story

Seven islands and everyone else's ambition

Mumbai was seven islands, marshy and unpromising, handed to the English in 1661 as part of a royal dowry. Two centuries of reclamation joined them, and the sea underneath still shows — in the curve of Marine Drive, in the tide climbing back over the causeway to Haji Ali, in the roads that flood every July. The Koli fishing communities were on the islands long before the dowry, and the city keeps their goddess's name: Mumbai, from Mumbadevi, whose temple still stands in the bazaar lanes near Zaveri Bazaar.

Almost nobody here is from here. The city's bargain is that it doesn't ask where you came from, only whether you can keep up — exhilarating for a week, and plainly visible on any suburban platform at nine in the morning.

02Culture

Read the buildings, then eat

The architecture is a timeline you can walk. Victorian Gothic along the university side of the Oval Maidan, Art Deco facing it across the grass — the two rows are a joint UNESCO listing — and Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj Terminus presiding over all of it, still a working station rather than a monument.

Food is the other archive. Irani cafés with bentwood chairs and rules posted on the wall, Gujarati thalis, Mangalorean fish, Bohri Mohalla after dark, and vada pav on nearly every corner. Eat standing up at least once.

For ten days in late August or September the city rearranges itself for Ganesh Chaturthi, its defining festival — neighbourhood pandals on every second corner, and immersion processions carrying Ganpati idols, some several storeys tall, through stopped traffic down to Girgaum Chowpatty. It is the one week Mumbai consents to run late.

03Practical guidance

The honest logistics

  • Best monthsNovember to February — dry, and about as cool as Mumbai gets. June to September is monsoon: dramatic and genuinely disruptive.
  • Getting thereChhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj International (BOM) connects almost everywhere; long-distance trains arrive at CSMT, Dadar and Bandra Terminus.
  • StayingColaba and Fort to walk the old city; Bandra for food and evenings. Near a station beats near a view.
  • Days neededThree: one for the old city on foot, one for Elephanta and the sea, one for whichever neighbourhood you liked.
  • Budget feelIndia's costliest city — comfortable from ₹4,000/day; hotel comfort in Colaba from ₹9,000/day.
04Local insight

What a Saint will tell you

Ride a local train, but off-peak and in the quiet direction — Churchgate to Bandra at eleven, not at nine. Elephanta's ferries leave from the Gateway and the caves close on Mondays, so take the first boat before the heat. Walk Marine Drive at high tide, when the spray comes over the wall. And the best meal you eat here will probably cost under ₹200.

05Responsible travel

Being a good guest

Dharavi tours divide people. If you go, choose an operator that employs residents and returns money to the area, and keep your camera in your bag. Refuse the plastic bag at every stall — this city's storm drains are where they end up. Tip the people who keep Mumbai running, generously and in cash.

06Next journey

Where this path continues

Mumbai and Kolkata are siblings — port cities built on other people's trade, both literary, both certain they are the real capital. Go and compare them. If you would rather have the coast without the crowd, south Goa is an overnight train away.
