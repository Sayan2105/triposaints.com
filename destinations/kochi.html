Kerala · South India

Kochi

A port built out of everyone else's leftovers

The Fort Kochi waterfront

01Story

A port made by a flood

Kochi became a harbour more or less by accident. A great flood in the fourteenth century silted up the old port of Muziris to the north and opened this lagoon instead. In the centuries that followed, the Portuguese, then the Dutch, then the British all turned up to argue over the pepper.

Each of them left a building and went home. Fort Kochi is what remains: a church from 1503 where Vasco da Gama was buried before his body was shipped to Lisbon, a synagogue from 1568 with hand-painted floor tiles brought from China, Dutch tombstones, cantilevered fishing nets of Chinese design. None of it is a theme park. People still live in all of it.

02Culture

The state that reads

Kerala's particular confidence shows up in small ways here — near-total literacy, a church procession and a communist rally on the same street in the same week, half the ferry reading a newspaper. The ferry is the best introduction to the city: a few rupees from Ernakulam jetty across to Fort Kochi, twenty minutes, no commentary.

Kathakali in its proper form runs most of a night; the version staged for visitors runs ninety minutes. Go anyway, but arrive an hour early to watch the make-up being built up layer by layer, which is the real performance. Kalaripayattu, the local martial art, is older and considerably less polite.

The port's oldest layer is still in daily use too. The Ernakulathappan temple — the city's guardian Shiva shrine, a short walk from the Ernakulam jetty — holds its eight-day Uthsavam each January, when caparisoned elephants and drum orchestras take over an otherwise commercial district. And across the water at Tripunithura, seat of the old Cochin royal family, the Sree Poornathrayeesa temple runs the Vrischikotsavam every November–December, with the family still turning up to open it.

03Practical guidance

The honest logistics

  • Best monthsNovember to February — dry and warm. June to September is serious monsoon, which some people come for on purpose.
  • Getting thereCochin International (COK), about 40 km out. Trains to Ernakulam Junction or Ernakulam Town from Chennai, Bengaluru and Mangaluru.
  • StayingFort Kochi for atmosphere and walking; Ernakulam for lower prices, better food and transport links.
  • Days neededTwo for Fort Kochi and Mattancherry; three if the Biennale is running.
  • Budget feelFrom ₹2,500/day; heritage homestays from around ₹6,000/day.
04Local insight

What a Saint will tell you

The Chinese nets are photographed constantly and catch very little now. The fish worth eating is at the stalls behind them, where you buy it raw and pay a nearby shack to cook it — agree both prices before anything touches oil. Mattancherry's warehouse lanes still smell of ginger and cardamom on a working morning. And cross the water at dusk by ferry rather than by taxi around.

05Responsible travel

Being a good guest

Fort Kochi is a residential neighbourhood, not a resort. Keep out of doorways, ask before photographing anyone at prayer, and dress for a conservative town rather than a beach. The synagogue and the churches are working places of worship — check the timings, and note the synagogue is closed on Saturdays.
