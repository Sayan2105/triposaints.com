Rajasthan · North India

Jaipur

Geometry, colour and a great deal of noise

The walled city, Jaipur

01Story

A city drawn on paper before it was built

Jaipur was designed, not grown. In 1727 Sawai Jai Singh II — astronomer as much as king — moved his capital down from the hills of Amber and had a scholar named Vidyadhar lay the whole thing out first: nine rectangular blocks, wide straight streets, walls last. Three hundred years later it still works. You can navigate the old city with your head up.

The pink came much later — a wash of terracotta ordered in 1876 for the Prince of Wales's visit, and never washed off. Nobody has quite let the city stop performing since.

02Culture

Almost everything here is still made by hand

Jaipur makes things. Blue pottery that contains no clay at all — quartz dough pressed into moulds, never thrown on a wheel — block-printed cotton from the villages at Bagru and Sanganer, coloured stones cut in workshops off Johari Bazaar that supply dealers on three continents. Walk the bazaars early, before the coaches, and you are mostly watching people at work.

The court left the rest. Jantar Mantar's stone instruments still tell the time; the City Palace still houses a family; Hawa Mahal's lattice of small windows was built so palace women could watch the street unseen. The palace kept its god close, too: Govind Dev Ji, the Krishna image Jai Singh brought down from Vrindavan, was installed in direct line with the Chandra Mahal so the king could see the deity from his rooms, and the temple's seven daily aartis still draw the old city's most reliable crowds. Breakfast is pyaaz kachori, dinner is laal maas, and in the monsoon everyone eats ghewar.

03Practical guidance

The honest logistics

  • Best monthsOctober to March. April to June pushes past 40°C and the city retreats into shaded courtyards.
  • Getting thereJaipur airport (JAI) has flights nationwide; the train from Delhi takes four to five hours, the expressway rather less.
  • StayingInside the walled city for bazaars at your door; Civil Lines or the Amber road for quiet. Restored havelis in both.
  • Days neededTwo days covers the forts and palaces; three lets you spend a morning in a workshop.
  • Budget feelComfortable from ₹2,500/day; heritage hotels from ₹7,000/day.
04Local insight

What a Saint will tell you

Reach Amber Fort by eight and walk up the ramp — the elephant rides are the one thing most Saints will steer you away from, and they will tell you why. Nahargarh at sunset beats any rooftop restaurant. Panna Meena ka Kund, the stepwell opposite Amber, is free and still quiet before the fort opens. Galtaji, in a ravine in the hills east of the walls, is a temple complex built around spring-fed kunds where pilgrims still bathe — go before ten, and count the monkeys as residents rather than an attraction. And in Bapu Bazaar the first price is an opening bid; your Saint will tell you what the second number should be.

05Responsible travel

Being a good guest

Buy from the workshop rather than the emporium — the difference in margin is enormous and it stays with the person who made the thing. Say no to the elephants. Water runs short here every summer, so treat a long hotel shower as the luxury it is, and ask before photographing anyone at work in the bazaars.
